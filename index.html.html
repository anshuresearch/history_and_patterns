Every generation thinks it
invented disruption.

What the printing press, railroads, and electricity actually did to jobs — and what the data says AI will really do.

Research-backed  •  Sources: NBER, MIT, Stanford, CEPR  •  ~8 min read

There is a version of the AI story that gets told at every conference, in every earnings call, in every breathless op-ed. It goes: this time is genuinely different, the pace of change is unprecedented, and anyone who isn't moving fast will be left behind. I've heard some version of this sentence for every major technology wave in my adult life. The sentence was also said about electricity. And railroads. And the printing press.

What's interesting is that the people saying it are always partially right — and systematically wrong about the part that matters most.

So I went back to the actual data. Not the hype. Not the counter-hype. The economic history research. Here's what three of history's biggest disruptions actually looked like on the ground — and what that pattern predicts about where we are today.

Chapter I

The Printing Press: The disruption nobody could measure

Gutenberg's press went live around 1440. The most immediate job market effect was brutal and local: scribes and copyists, people who had spent decades mastering hand-copying manuscripts, saw their livelihood collapse within a generation. That part of the story everyone knows.

Here's the part that gets left out. Economists at CEPR studied 200 European cities between 1450 and 1600 and found that cities adopting the printing press showed economic growth up to 60 percentage points higher than cities that didn't. The press didn't just replace scribes. It created publishers, booksellers, typesetters, editors, journalists, paper mills, ink manufacturers — an entire industrial ecosystem that hadn't existed before.

Printing Press: City Economic Growth Gap
Cumulative economic growth differential — cities with vs. without press adoption (1450–1600) • Source: CEPR / Dittmar (2011)
Source: Dittmar, J. (2011). "Information Technology and Economic Change: The Impact of the Printing Press." Quarterly Journal of Economics, 126(3).

But here's the problem with that 60-point number. The scribes didn't get any of it. The gains went to the cities, to the merchants, to the entrepreneurs who built new businesses on top of the new technology. The people the technology displaced were mostly not the people the technology enriched. That gap — between aggregate gains and individual outcomes — is something the disruption narrative almost never accounts for.

There's a second uncomfortable finding. Despite the printing press's obvious civilizational impact, economists could not find it in aggregate productivity statistics for over a century. The Solow Paradox — the observation that you can see computers everywhere except in the productivity data — was actually preceded by what you might call the Gutenberg Paradox. Transformative technologies routinely hide from macroeconomic measurement for decades.

"The printing press had very far-reaching consequences — but economists struggled to find any evidence of its impact in measures of aggregate productivity or per capita income."

CEPR / VoxEU — Dittmar (2011)
Chapter II

Railroads: The jobs were real. So was the inequality.

By 1880, approximately 400,000 men — nearly 2.5% of the entire US workforce — worked directly in the railroad industry. And that's before you count the downstream jobs: steel workers, coal miners, telegraph operators, accountants, station managers, hotel keepers along the new routes. The railroads didn't just move goods. They created the first large-scale American corporations, the first professional management class, and the first standardized national accounting systems.

They also reduced freight costs dramatically, which let Midwestern farmers reach East Coast markets for the first time, which expanded agricultural output, which fed industrial growth. The multiplier effects were enormous.

US Railroad Workforce Growth (1850–1900)
Estimated direct railroad employment as % of total US workforce • Source: American Yawp / BLS Historical Data
Source: "Western Economic Expansion: Railroads and Cattle," American Yawp; US Census Bureau historical workforce data.

And yet. World Bank research examining the railroad expansion found that it explained roughly 60% of the observed increase in the real estate wealth share of the top 10% between 1850 and 1870. More jobs, yes. More total wealth, yes. But the gains were heavily concentrated at the top of the distribution, faster than most workers could adjust. Towns that got the railroad boomed. Towns that didn't, many of them simply stopped growing.

This is the part of the railroad story that the "technology creates jobs" summary skips. The net job number was positive. The distribution of those jobs was deeply unequal. Both things were true simultaneously.

Chapter III

Electricity: The 30-year lag nobody talks about

This is the case I find most useful for thinking about AI, because it's the most specific and the most counterintuitive.

Edison built the first electric generating stations in New York and London in 1881. By 1900, electric motors still accounted for less than 5% of factory mechanical drive. Twenty years after the technology existed, it had barely penetrated the thing it was supposed to transform. And when it did start entering factories — here's the key detail — productivity didn't improve. For nearly 30 years.

Stanford economist Paul David studied this in his 1990 paper "The Dynamo and the Computer" and found the reason. Factories didn't redesign around electricity. They just replaced the steam engine with a dynamo and kept everything else the same: the same central shaft, the same belt-and-pulley layout, the same multi-story building structure, the same supervision logic. Same operating model, different power source.

Electrification vs. Productivity: The 30-Year Gap
Electric motor share of US factory power vs. measurable productivity gains (indexed) • Source: Paul David (1990), Warren Devine (1983)
Source: David, P.A. (1990). "The Dynamo and the Computer." American Economic Review, 80(2). Devine, W. (1983). "From Shafts to Wires." Journal of Economic History, 43.

It wasn't until the 1920s, when manufacturers finally asked "why does a factory even have this shape?" that things changed. They moved to single-story layouts organized around production flow. They gave individual workers their own small electric motors. Output exploded. But the explosion came 40 years after the technology was available — and only after someone fundamentally rethought the organizational model, not just the power source.

"Steam-powered factories had to be arranged on the logic of the driveshaft. Electricity meant you could organise factories on the logic of a production line. That changed everything — but it took 30 years to figure out."

Tim Harford, 50 Things That Made the Modern Economy — citing Paul David (1990)
Chapter IV

AI: We are currently in the steam-engine-swap phase

Now look at where we actually are with AI, in the data, not in the press releases.

90%
of firms in NBER survey report AI had no impact on employment or productivity over 3 years
1.5h
average AI usage per week among executives who reported "using AI" at work
$250B
corporate AI investment in 2024, with no matching signal in GDP, wages, or employment data

A 2024 NBER study tracked 25,000 workers across Denmark and found virtually no measurable impact on wages, working hours, or employment — two years into the ChatGPT era, with firms actively deploying AI tools. Apollo's chief economist Torsten Slok put it plainly: "AI is everywhere except in the incoming macroeconomic data. You don't see AI in the employment data, productivity data, or inflation data."

Nobel laureate Daron Acemoglu's 2024 MIT study projected a 0.5% productivity increase over the next decade. He said at the time: "I don't think we should belittle 0.5% in 10 years. That's better than zero. But it's disappointing relative to the promises."

This is not a sign that AI doesn't work. It's the exact same pattern as the electricity paradox. Most companies right now are doing what factory owners did in 1890: plugging AI into the existing workflow. Same org chart, same processes, same decision logic — just with a new tool in specific slots. Same operating model, different power source.

MIT research on US manufacturing firms published in January 2026 actually showed that AI adoption causes a measurable short-term productivity decline before the gains arrive — what the researchers call a "J-curve." The professor leading the study said it plainly: "AI isn't plug-and-play. It requires systemic change, and that process introduces friction."

The Pattern

What the three cases actually have in common

Iphase

The technology arrives — and does less than expected

Printing press displaces scribes but doesn't show in aggregate productivity. Electric dynamos replace steam engines but productivity stays flat. ChatGPT gets adopted widely but doesn't appear in employment or wage data. This phase is consistently misread as failure.

IIphase

Someone asks the wrong question — and pays for it

Factory owners in 1890 asked "how do we add electricity?" instead of "how would we build this factory if electricity had always existed?" Companies today are mostly asking the first question about AI. The ones asking the second question will look prescient in 20 years.

IIIphase

The gains arrive — distributed unevenly and faster than people expect

When the productivity gains from electricity finally appeared in the 1920s, they were large and rapid. The cities that thrived after the printing press weren't the ones that managed the transition gently — they were the ones that reorganized around the new reality. The railroad wealth gains went disproportionately to the top 10%, and they went there fast.

IVphase

The disruption narrative gets rewritten — to make it sound inevitable

We now talk about the industrial revolution as a tide that lifted all boats. It did, eventually. But the weavers displaced by textile machines in 1810 didn't become factory owners in 1850. The timeline of "new jobs created" rarely matched the timeline of "old jobs destroyed." Both timelines were real. Only one gets remembered.

The uncomfortable conclusion

The disruption is real. The productivity gains will come. The pattern is consistent across 600 years of economic history, and there's no particular reason to think AI breaks it.

But the pattern also says: the gains, when they arrive, will not distribute the way the people who needed them most required. That's not an opinion. That's what happened with the press, the railroad, and electricity — every time, without exception.

The last part is the one nobody puts in the LinkedIn post. Until now.
